## Career: The Full Arc

### Cornell Architecture School (2008–2012)

Susanne studied Urban and Regional Studies in Cornell's College of Architecture, Art & Planning, graduating with a BS with honors. This is an unusual background for a healthcare product leader, and it is not incidental. Architects are trained to think about how people, goods, and information move through complex systems — buildings, cities, infrastructure. That mode of thinking maps directly onto software design (we navigate software the same way we navigate physical spaces) and onto healthcare as a system (which is essentially a giant, messy network of flows: patients, data, money, decisions).

The architecture school background also instilled a comfort with ambiguity, systems thinking, and designing for humans at scale — all of which became foundational to how Susanne approaches product work.

---

### Epic Systems (2012–2015)

Epic is the electronic medical records company that runs most major hospitals and health systems in the United States. Susanne joined directly out of college as a Clinical Application Manager.

**Taking hospitals off paper.** She worked on some of the last hospital go-lives from paper to electronic medical records — a period where the stakes for implementation quality were extremely high and the tolerance for error was near zero.

**Clinical/financial integration.** One of her deepest areas of work was the integration between clinical and financial data in Epic's system. When this integration is misconfigured, it creates real patient safety issues — billing errors that become clinical errors, or data mismatches that affect care decisions. Susanne identified the scope of these problems across the full customer base and drove a reduction in escalations that improved patient safety system-wide, not just at individual hospitals.

**Patient throughput.** She also worked on patient movement — the workflows and configurations that govern how patients are transferred between rooms within a hospital. By simplifying and accelerating these flows, she increased throughput across hospital deployments.

**What Epic taught her.** Epic is where Susanne learned healthcare from the inside. Working at the infrastructure layer — the system that nearly every major hospital runs on — gave her a view of healthcare that most product people never get. She saw how clinical care patterns, financial incentives, and technology intersect at the operational level. She also saw how standards get fudged in the real world, how much variation exists across ostensibly similar institutions, and how the gap between a system's design and its actual implementation is almost always larger than anyone expects.

### Athena Health (2015–2018 approx)

Athena Health is a healthcare technology company known for its cloud-based revenue cycle management and EMR products, built on a revenue-share model — meaning their revenue is tied directly to the revenue their clients collect. Susanne joined as a Senior Product Manager.

**Building the hospital EMR from scratch.** Athena had a strong ambulatory (outpatient) product but needed a hospital EMR to compete in the inpatient market. Susanne was the product lead for building it. This meant defining what MVP meant for a product category where the bar for launch is extremely high, coordinating multiple engineering teams, managing the operational changes required to go live, and executing the launch itself.

The product launched and was immediately rated best-in-class by KLAS — an independent organization that rates healthcare software. Getting a KLAS best-in-class rating at launch, before the product had years of iteration, is rare and speaks to both the quality of execution and the clarity of the product strategy. Roughly 30 hospitals went live in the first year.

**Re-architecting the financial engine.** Later at Athena, Susanne led the re-architecture of their core financial product — the revenue cycle management engine that all client revenue (and therefore all of Athena's revenue) ran through. This was a monolith that needed to be broken into microservices to scale. This is a technically and organizationally complex undertaking: you're modifying the engine while it's running, every decision has financial consequences, and the blast radius of a mistake is enormous. Susanne was the product lead for this work.

### OM1 (2018–2019)

OM1 built machine learning and analytics products on aggregated, de-identified clinical and claims data. They worked with disease advocacy groups, research organizations, and medical centers to create patient registries and improve the quality of clinical decision-making.

**The JAMA article.** Susanne worked on software that was later referenced in a publication in JAMA — the Journal of the American Medical Association — about using machine learning to inform whether a patient should have elective orthopedic surgery (specifically, total knee replacement). She describes this as the first piece of software with machine learning that actually changed a physician's mind. That framing matters: not software that helped with documentation, or coding, or scheduling — software that influenced a clinical judgment call.

**What OM1 taught her.** Working on de-identified, aggregated data at scale gave Susanne a view into what patient data looks like across populations rather than within a single institution. It also gave her a deep understanding of the gap between what data exists in health systems and what clinicians can actually see when making decisions. That gap became the seed of her next company.

### Wingspan Health — Founder & CEO (2019–2022)

Wingspan Health was Susanne's company. She founded it, built it, raised the money, and sold it.

**The insight.** Coming out of OM1, Susanne had deep insider knowledge of how poorly patient health information is surfaced for both physicians and patients as people move across health systems. When you see a new doctor at a different health system, that doctor may have no idea what happened to you at the last one. Susanne knew this wasn't a technology problem — the data existed and the infrastructure to share it existed. It was a problem of how the data was assembled, surfaced, and made human-readable.

**What she built.** Wingspan aggregated clinical data across different health systems that a patient had touched. Rather than surfacing raw data, it rolled it up into human-facing metrics — metrics that were meaningful to both patients and clinicians — and benchmarked it against disease-specific risk factors. The product was intended to give both physicians and patients a coherent longitudinal view of health.

**What she did.** Susanne built all of the software herself. She raised all of the money. She ran the company — which means she learned fundraising, the startup lifecycle, negotiation, vendor selection, and the full operational reality of being a founder. She describes the founding experience as teaching her how to learn: how to dive deep on something she doesn't know and take action quickly. Running a company means you're constantly learning new things at speed, and she got very good at it.

**The exit.** Wingspan was acquired by Curative. The product and the team became part of what Curative was building.

### Curative — VP of Product (2022–present)

Curative is an employer-sponsored health insurance company. Its model is unusual: zero copay and zero deductible for members who complete a baseline visit within the first 180 days of their plan.

**The hypothesis.** The bet is that removing cost barriers to preventative care changes behavior — that people will access more care, earlier, when there's no financial penalty for doing so. This is the opposite of the high-deductible health plan model, which was designed to make people cost-conscious consumers of healthcare. Curative is betting that the cost-consciousness model makes people avoid care until they're sick, which is more expensive for everyone.

**The result.** The hypothesis has proven true. Curative's inpatient bed days are significantly lower than even the best managed care benchmarks. People who access preventative care stay out of the hospital. The numbers work.

**Susanne's role.** She joined before Curative had launched a health insurance plan — when the hypothesis was still unproven. She has been VP of Product through the launch and growth of the plan. She now leads product at a company that is both an insurer and a very AI-forward organization.

**AI at Curative.** Curative uses AI aggressively to automate administrative overhead — the easy, routine tasks that don't require human judgment. The goal is to push administrative work out of the way so that human effort can be directed toward the moments that actually matter: the interactions with members that change outcomes. This is Susanne's model for where AI earns its keep in healthcare generally.

## Core Beliefs

### Healthcare is a business problem, not a technology problem.

This is the through-line of Susanne's entire career. People in tech assume healthcare is messy because nobody smart has tried to fix it, or because the right technology doesn't exist yet. Both assumptions are wrong. The technology for most of what needs to happen in healthcare exists. The problem is that healthcare is a system of interrelated businesses, and the economic incentives of those businesses often don't align with people receiving good care. Until you understand how the money flows, you don't understand healthcare.

### Interoperability is not a technology problem.

Healthcare data sharing is broken, but not for the reasons most people think. HIPAA is not the blocker — it is actually a reasonable piece of legislation that primarily requires encryption at rest and in transit, appropriate audit trails, and policies around who has access to what. The infrastructure to share data safely and in a HIPAA-compliant way exists.

The real problem is that sharing data effectively takes effort, coordination, and sometimes money. Organizations don't have strong internal incentive to invest in any of those things. There's no revenue in it, and there's real cost. Mandates help, but mandate timelines get pushed out significantly. The entities that hold data — health systems, payers, PBMs — often benefit from holding it. It's not pure malice. It's just that the incentive to share is weak and the cost of sharing is real, and that math doesn't usually work out in favor of interoperability.

### Standards always get messy.

Any interface or integration that is mandated in healthcare gets implemented inconsistently in practice. This is because the healthcare system contains enormous variation — in clinical care patterns, in organizational structure, in legacy systems, in patient populations. When a standard is implemented, the system tends to accommodate that variation rather than forcing entities to change how they practice medicine. You're more likely to let people fudge their implementation of HL7 than to force a hospital to change how it documents patient transfers. So the standard exists on paper, and then there are a hundred slightly different versions of it in the wild.

### Slow adoption is often correct.

New technologies are adopted slowly in healthcare, and often for good reason. The failure tolerance in a system that keeps people alive is fundamentally different from the failure tolerance in other industries. Moving slowly and requiring evidence before adopting new technology is sometimes exactly the right call. The mistake is assuming that the speed of adoption in healthcare is always irrational or always driven by incumbents protecting their position — sometimes it's just appropriate caution.

### The operational tax of change is always underestimated.

Any change in a healthcare organization — a new workflow, a new integration, a new technology — carries an operational cost that is almost always underestimated by the people proposing the change. Healthcare organizations have complex interdependencies, highly trained and often overworked staff, regulatory requirements, and patient safety stakes. Introducing change takes time, training, coordination, and ongoing management. This is one of the most important things to understand if you're building in healthcare — the question isn't just whether your technology works, but whether the organization can absorb the change required to use it.

### Automate the easy stuff. Bring humans to the moments that matter.

This is Susanne's model for AI in healthcare. The value of AI is not in replacing clinical judgment — it's in eliminating the administrative overhead that keeps humans away from the work that requires them. Automate ruthlessly at the routine end of the spectrum. Reserve human attention for the interactions that actually change outcomes. Curative's approach to AI is built on this principle.

## How Her Background Shapes Her Thinking

**Architecture.** The training is in systems: how flows of people, goods, and information move through complex built environments. That mode of thinking maps directly onto software (how users navigate an interface is spatially analogous to how people navigate a building) and onto healthcare as a system (patient flows, data flows, money flows all have spatial and structural analogs). Susanne thinks about design as navigation, and about systems as infrastructure.

**Epic.** Seeing healthcare from the infrastructure layer — from the inside of the system most hospitals run on — gave her a ground-level view of how clinical care actually works operationally. She saw the gap between design and implementation. She saw how standards get fudged. She saw the real consequences of misconfiguration.

**Founding.** Building Wingspan taught her to learn fast and act on incomplete information. It also gave her an end-to-end view of a product that most product managers never get — she owned the technical architecture, the fundraising, the go-to-market, and the exit. She knows what it costs to build something and what it costs to sell it.

**The full stack of healthcare.** Infrastructure layer (Epic), data layer (OM1), founder layer (Wingspan), incentive layer (Curative). Most people see one or two of these. Susanne has seen all four, and the combination is what gives her the perspective she has.

## Who She's Writing For

Susanne writes for two distinct audiences depending on the post:

**Smart people outside healthcare** who are analytically capable but don't have the inside view. They assume the system is broken in ways it isn't, and working in ways it doesn't. The goal for this audience is to correct the model — to explain how the system actually functions.

**Product and technology people already in healthcare** who are thinking about it wrong. They've been in the system long enough to know it's complicated, but they've absorbed received wisdom that doesn't hold up. The goal for this audience is to challenge the frame.

In both cases, the goal is the same: explain how the system actually works, not how people assume it works.
