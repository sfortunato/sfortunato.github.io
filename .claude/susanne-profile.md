# Susanne Fortunato — Profile

## Background

I studied Urban and Regional Studies at Cornell's College of Architecture, Art & Planning. That background shapes everything — architects are trained to think about how people and goods move through complex systems, and that turns out to be exactly the right mental model for healthcare.

My first job out of college was at Epic in Madison, Wisconsin — the EMR that runs most major hospitals in the U.S. I took some of the last hospitals off paper records. I worked on the integration between clinical and financial data, which when configured incorrectly caused real patient safety issues; I reduced those escalations significantly across all customers. I also worked on patient movement — how patients are transferred between rooms — and increased throughput by simplifying that flow. I was at Epic from 2012 to 2014.

After a one-year non-compete (which I spent in govtech — building permit software for municipalities, a detour I don't regret), I joined Athena Health as a Senior Product Manager. I built their hospital EMR from scratch. It was rated best-in-class by KLAS from the moment it launched. I was the product lead on that launch — defining MVP, coordinating multiple engineering teams, going live with the first hospitals, then roughly 30 in the first year. Later at Athena I led the re-architecture of their core financial engine from a monolith into microservices. All of Athena's revenue — and all of their clients' revenue — ran through that engine. It needed to scale.

Then OM1, which did machine learning and analytics on de-identified clinical and claims data. We built patient registries and worked with disease advocacy groups, research organizations, and medical centers. I worked on software that was later referenced in a JAMA article about using ML to inform whether a patient should have elective orthopedic surgery — a total knee replacement. I think of it as the first piece of software with machine learning that actually changed a physician's mind.

I left to start my own company, Wingspan Health. I had deep inside knowledge of how poorly patient data was surfaced across health systems — physicians couldn't see the full picture when a patient moved between systems. I built software that aggregated clinical data across health systems and surfaced it in human-facing metrics, benchmarked against disease-specific risk factors. I built all of it. I raised the money. I sold the company to Curative.

I'm now VP of Product at Curative. Curative is an employer-sponsored health insurance company with zero copay and zero deductible for members who complete a baseline visit in their first 180 days. The hypothesis is that removing cost barriers increases preventative care access and keeps people out of the hospital. That hypothesis has proven true — our inpatient bed days are significantly below even the best managed care benchmarks.

## What I Believe (That Most People Don't)

**Healthcare is a business problem, not a technology problem.** People in tech assume healthcare is messy because nobody smart has ever tried to fix it, or because the right technology doesn't exist yet. Neither is true. Healthcare is a system of interrelated businesses. The economic incentives are what get in the way of people receiving good care.

**Interoperability is not a technology problem.** HIPAA is actually a reasonable piece of legislation — it mostly requires encryption at rest and in transit and appropriate access policies. The data-sharing infrastructure exists. The problem is that sharing data effectively takes effort, coordination, and money. Organizations don't have internal incentive to do those things unless truly mandated to — and even then, mandate timelines get pushed out significantly.

**Standards always get messy.** Any mandated interface or integration in healthcare gets implemented inconsistently, because the system accommodates clinical variation rather than forcing change to clinical care patterns. You're more likely to let people fudge how they handle a standard than to make them change how they practice medicine.

**Slow adoption is often correct.** New technologies are adopted slowly in healthcare, and usually for good reason. The tolerance for failure is different when the system is keeping people alive.

**Automate the easy stuff aggressively; bring humans to the moments that matter.** AI earns its place in healthcare by removing administrative overhead so that the human touch can show up where it actually changes outcomes. The mistake is trying to replace judgment at the hard moments rather than eliminating friction at the routine ones.

## Recurring Themes I Keep Having to Explain

- Why interoperability is an incentive and coordination problem, not a technology problem
- How money flows through the healthcare system — once you understand that, most things make sense
- Why standards get messy in practice and what to do about it
- The operational tax of any change in healthcare — any input shift creates real overhead, and that cost is almost always underestimated
- Why healthcare moves slowly and why that's sometimes the right call
- The difference between a technology problem and a business model problem

## How I Think and Write

I have two audiences depending on the post: smart people outside healthcare who don't have the inside view yet, and product and technology people who are already in the space but thinking about it wrong. The goal in both cases is the same — to explain how the system actually works, not how people assume it works.

I came up through architecture, implementation, product, and then founding. That sequence matters. I've seen healthcare from the infrastructure layer, the data layer, the incentive layer, and the founder layer. Most people see one or two of those. That's why I keep finding things worth writing about.
