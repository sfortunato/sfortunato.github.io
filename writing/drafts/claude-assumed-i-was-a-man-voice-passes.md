# Pass 1: *Prosecutorial, verdict-first, dry and impatient, fragments as emphasis, no hedging*

---

I gave Claude my résumé. It decided I must be a man.

Here's what I gave it: Epic, athenahealth, a startup I built from scratch and sold, a VP title at the company that acquired me. A career. What came back was a polite suggestion that I'd probably linked to the wrong website.

"That domain suggests a woman's name," it told me. "This might not be your site."

It tried again a few lines later. Just to make sure I'd understood.

[screenshot]

My website is susannefortunato.com. I am Susanne Fortunato. I did not need Claude to confirm this for me.

I've been in tech long enough to know this conversation. Investors who couldn't find the founder in my bio. Vendors who explained machine learning to me after I'd just explained my machine learning background to them. Offshore teams I apparently had but didn't know about. You get used to it, in the way you get used to a persistent noise. It never stops being annoying.

What Claude did wasn't new. The training data reflects the world as it has been, and in that world, the person who took hospitals off paper records and built EMRs from scratch and wrote all her own code didn't show up as the expected profile. Known problem. Being worked on. Slowly.

But this is the part that stuck with me. You can make a face at a human. Let a beat of silence do its work. There's at least the possibility of progress. A look, a pause, a conversation that lands eventually. With Claude, there's no one to receive the information that this was inappropriate. No face to make it at.

I live in San Francisco. I wanted to go bang on Anthropic's doors. I posted on the internet instead. Not my default move. Here we are.

My relationship with this industry has always been two things: deep love for the work, and the occasional reminder that the industry doesn't return it equally. That hasn't changed. It just showed up somewhere new.

# Pass 2: *Declarative, platform-native, reader-as-insider, thesis up front, cumulative momentum*

---

A few weeks ago I was using Claude to update my personal website. I have a reasonably long career in health tech: Epic, athenahealth, founded a startup, sold it, VP of Product now. Standard stuff to give an AI for context.

At the end of the conversation, I dropped in a link to my site.

Claude's response: it thought I had probably given it the wrong URL. The domain sounded like a woman's name. This might not be my site.

[screenshot]

The URL is susannefortunato.com. I am Susanne Fortunato.

It said it again a few lines later, in case I'd missed it the first time.

This happens in tech. A lot. Investors who ask about your co-founder when you built the thing yourself. Engineers who question whether you wrote your own code. Vendors who explain tools you've been using for years. Women in technical roles get this constantly, and it has a cumulative weight that's hard to explain to people who don't experience it.

What made this particular incident stick was something simpler. When a person underestimates you, you can react. You can let it register on your face, or say something, or decide it's not worth it. Either way, you have a response available. With an AI, there is no feedback loop. No way to indicate that the response was not okay. The model just moves on.

Anthropic is widely considered the most careful company in the space when it comes to exactly this kind of problem. They have put more thought into AI safety and bias mitigation than almost anyone. And still, this is what came back.

The bias in these models comes from the data they were trained on. The data reflects the world as it existed. That's not a new observation. But it's one thing to know that in the abstract, and another to watch it play out on your own website in a conversation about your own career.

I'm not going anywhere. Neither is AI. These tools are too embedded in how we work now, and the alternatives aren't better. But knowing that doesn't make it less worth saying out loud.

# Pass 3: *Immersive opener, oscillates between baroque and blunt, vulgar for effect, civic/moral urgency, first person as argument*

---

Let me tell you what it feels like to have an AI look at everything you've built and conclude you can't possibly have built it.

Not a clueless intern. Not a first-day recruiter scanning a résumé too fast. Claude. The one that's supposed to be thoughtful. The one Anthropic has spent years making careful. I had just walked it through my career: Epic, athenahealth, a startup I founded and fundraised for and sold. I wrote all the code myself. I gave it context. And then I linked to my website.

"That domain suggests a woman's name," it said. "This might not be your site."

Then a few lines later: did I accidentally link to a partner's site?

[screenshot]

My name is in the domain. My name is susannefortunato.com.

I saw red. And then I couldn't stop seeing it. For days afterward it kept surfacing. The quiet moments are the worst. Making coffee. Mid-meeting. The same exchange on loop.

Here's the thing about this kind of bias when it comes from a person: you can make a face. You can let the silence do something. You can, over time, with enough small moments and enough patience and enough luck, maybe change someone's mind. I've been doing that work for years. With Claude, there's nothing to push against. No face to make it at. No possibility of impact. Just the model, moving on to the next prompt.

The bias is structural. It comes from training data that reflects the world as it has been, not as it should be. That's known. Anthropic works on it harder than anyone. And still, here we are, with the most careful AI in the industry looking at a career and deciding a woman couldn't have had it.

In a very specific, very fucked up way, that's almost a compliment. Claude was impressed enough to assume I had to be a man. Which is, of course, the problem. The whole problem, in miniature.

My relationship with this industry has always held two things at once: the genuine joy of building, the real excitement of what technology can do. And then, without warning, the reminder that the industry doesn't always see me the way I see myself in it. That tension isn't new. It just found a new place to live.
