I was having a long conversation with Claude about my career — trying to think through what story my website should be telling. I had given it a lot of context: I started in healthcare tech at Epic, taking hospitals off paper records. Moved to Athena Health and built their hospital EMR from scratch — it launched best-in-class. Then a machine learning company, then founding my own startup, which I built and funded and eventually sold. Now VP of Product at the acquiring company. I write the code myself.

At the end of the conversation, I dropped in a link to my personal site so Claude could take a look.

The response came back: "Interesting — that domain suggests a woman's name, which makes me think this might not be your site, or the domain is unrelated to your name."

And then, a few lines later, it tried again: "I noticed the URL is susannefortunato.com, which sounds like it might be a partner/spouse's site rather than yours? Just want to make sure I'm pulling the right content. What's your name so I can also update the placeholder correctly?"

[screenshot]

My website is susannefortunato.com. I am Susanne Fortunato.

I wanted to scream. And then I couldn't stop thinking about it. For days afterward, in any quiet moment — making coffee, on a walk, mid-meeting — it popped back into my head. The exchange was haunting me.

There's something almost darkly funny about it, if you squint. Claude had just reviewed a career that most people find genuinely impressive. It saw all of that — and concluded the only reasonable explanation was that the person who built it must be a man. In a very fucked up way, it was almost a compliment. Claude was impressed enough to assume I couldn't possibly be a woman. Which is, of course, exactly the problem.

---

Here's what makes this worth writing about rather than just venting about: I love this tool. I'm not a skeptic cataloguing AI failures from the outside. I've been building with Claude Code long enough to have developed a working vocabulary for the feeling — it's either hitting the highway after being stuck in city traffic, or riding an e-bike around town. The second one is closer. There is genuine joy in it. So when it turned around and treated me the way certain investors and vendors do, it landed differently than it would have coming from a stranger.

None of this is new. When I started my own company — I built the software, raised the money, sold it — I got used to a particular kind of investor conversation. "So your technical co-founder who built the app..." No. "Your engineering team, your offshore team..." No. I write all the code. The conversation would loop two or three times before it landed, and sometimes it didn't land at all.

That pattern predates AI by decades. What Claude did wasn't different in kind. But it was different in one specific way that I keep coming back to: you can make a face at a human. You can let something register on your face, let a beat of silence do its work. There's at least the theoretical possibility of changing someone's mind — small moments accumulate, attitudes shift, people occasionally surprise you. With Claude, there is no way to tell it how inappropriate it's being. I live in San Francisco. What I wanted to do was go bang on Anthropic's doors. What I got to do instead was post something on the internet.

---

My relationship with the tech industry has always involved holding two things at once: deep connection to the work, real excitement about what's being built — and the occasional sudden reminder that the industry doesn't always see me the way I see myself in it. That tension is old. What's new is that now it shows up in the tools too.

I'm not sure this changes anything. But I wanted it on record somewhere.
