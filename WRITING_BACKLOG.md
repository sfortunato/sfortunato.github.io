| The incentive problem: why healthcare keeps hiring the wrong technology | idea | Smart people have been trying to fix healthcare with technology for decades. The system isn't broken because the right software doesn't exist. It's broken because the economic incentives don't point toward good care. |
| Interoperability is not a technology problem | idea | We have the infrastructure. HIPAA is not the blocker. The reason healthcare data doesn't flow is that sharing it costs real effort and money, and the organizations holding it don't have sufficient incentive to do it. That's an organizational problem, not a technical one. |
| HIPAA is not the villain | idea | Most people in tech blame HIPAA for healthcare's data problems. HIPAA is actually a reasonable law — encryption, audit trails, access policies. The data isn't locked up because of regulation. It's locked up because the organizations holding it don't have a reason to let it go. |
| Why standards always get messy in healthcare | idea | Every mandated integration in healthcare gets implemented in a hundred slightly different ways. The reason isn't incompetence — it's that the system accommodates clinical variation rather than forcing change to how medicine is actually practiced. |
| The operational tax nobody talks about | idea | Introducing new technology into a healthcare organization costs more than the technology. The real price is the organizational change required — training, coordination, workflow disruption — and that cost is almost always underestimated by the people proposing the change. |
| Why slow adoption in healthcare is often correct | idea | Everyone in tech finds healthcare's pace of change frustrating. But the failure tolerance in a system that keeps people alive is fundamentally different. Sometimes slow adoption isn't incumbents protecting position — it's appropriate caution, and it's worth being honest about that. |
| The right model for AI in healthcare | idea | AI earns its place in healthcare by eliminating administrative overhead, not by replacing clinical judgment. Automate the routine stuff aggressively so that human attention can show up at the moments that actually change outcomes. |
| What the zero-copay bet gets right | idea | High-deductible health plans make people cost-conscious consumers of healthcare. The problem is that avoiding care until you're sick is more expensive for everyone. The Curative hypothesis — that removing cost barriers increases preventative access — has proven true in the numbers. |
| On reading the data flow before you build anything | idea | Before you build anything in healthcare, follow the money. How does revenue get generated? Who captures it? Where does it go? Most product decisions that turn out to be wrong were made without answering these questions first. |
| What I learned building a health startup alone | idea | Building and selling Wingspan taught me how to learn fast and act on incomplete information. It also taught me the difference between knowing what to build and knowing what the system will actually let you build — and those are very different problems. |
| What working at Epic actually teaches you | idea | Working at Epic is a residency in how healthcare actually operates. You learn the gap between how systems are designed and how they get implemented. You learn why variation exists. You learn that the problem in front of you is almost never the one you were told it was. |
| The first software that actually changed a physician's mind | idea | Most healthcare technology works around clinical decisions without changing them. The machine learning work I did at OM1 on elective orthopedic surgery was different — it actually shifted what a physician decided to do. Here's what that required and why it's harder than it sounds. |
